How Macro Works

Inflation, interest rates, bonds, gold and the dollar are all links in one chain. Pick a trigger below and follow what happens next — the market's first move, what policymakers do to counter it, and where it leads over time.

Start here — the one big lever

Almost everything reacts to one thing

A central bank (like the US Federal Reserve) has essentially one job and one main tool: keep prices stable by moving the interest rate. Most of macro is just the knock-on effects of that lever moving.

🔥 Too hot

Inflation runs high

The economy is overheating — too much money chasing too few goods. The central bank raises rates to cool it down.

🧊 Too cold

Growth stalls

Demand is weak and jobs are at risk. The central bank cuts rates to warm things up and get money moving again.

Bonds, stocks, gold and the dollar are mostly reacting to where that rate is going. Once you can predict the lever, you can reason about the rest.

The one rule people trip on

Bond price and yield are a seesaw

A bond pays a fixed amount. If new bonds start paying higher interest (yield), nobody wants the old lower-paying one at full price — so its price falls until the return matches. And the reverse: when yields drop, existing bond prices rise.

So “bonds selling off” and “yields spiking” describe the same event. “Bond rates higher” = bond prices lower.

Why it never really ends

The cycle repeats

These moves aren't one-offs — they loop. Today's cure becomes tomorrow's cause.

01
Easy money
Low rates, cheap borrowing. Spending and investment pick up.
→
02
Growth & inflation build
Demand runs hot; prices and wages start climbing.
→
03
Central bank tightens
Rates rise to cool inflation. Bonds & stocks come under pressure.
→
04
Slowdown
Costlier money slows the economy — sometimes into recession.
↻ …which brings rate cuts and easy money again — back to step 01.

How to read this. These are typical tendencies, not laws — markets price in expectations ahead of events, and any single day can defy the pattern. The biggest swing factor is real yields (interest rate minus inflation): gold and risky assets care about those more than headline rates. This is an educational explainer, not investment advice.
