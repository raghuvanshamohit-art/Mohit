01The correction

Two dials, not a seesaw.

You said: when supply rises, demand falls; when supply falls, demand rises. The price conclusion is right — but the mechanism has one flaw worth fixing, because the whole system rests on it.

Supply and demand don’t cause each other. They are two independent forces, and price is simply the level where they meet. Demand can sit perfectly still while supply drains away — and price still climbs, because it’s the ratio of sellers to buyers that moves price, not one dial pushing the other. Keep them as two separate dials, never one seesaw.

The stock-specific twist: unlike vegetables or crude, a company can’t print new shares when buyers arrive. Its float is roughly fixed. So here, supply = shares people will sell right now, demand = shares people want to buy right now — and extra supply only enters through IPOs, promoter selling and lock-up expiries, and shrinks through buybacks.

02The key insight

One phrase, two meanings.

When you say “the supply and demand of a sector,” you can mean two very different things. Both are real, both are measurable — and the best trades come when they agree.

Lens A — the real economy

Supply & demand of the product

Every sector sells something: Metals sell steel & aluminium, Energy sells crude & gas, Sugar sells sugar. When that product turns scarce, its price rises → revenue and margins expand → the stocks rise. This is your idea, exactly. Scarcity of the product → the sector rises. It tells you why a sector should move.

Lens B — the money

Supply & demand for the shares

Separately, big money rotates between sectors across the cycle. When capital floods into a sector’s shares faster than sellers appear, the index rises — whatever the product is doing. This shows up as relative strength, breadth and fund flows. It tells you whether the money is actually moving there yet.

03The trap

Same scarcity, opposite effect.

Scarcity helps whoever sells the scarce thing and hurts whoever buys it. So every sector must be tagged a producer or a consumer of its key commodity — and the sign flips. This is the single rule that breaks naïve versions of the idea.

Crude oil turns scarce →
Rises Upstream Energy (ONGC, Oil India) — they sell crude
Falls Aviation, paints, tyres — they buy crude
Steel turns scarce →
Rises Metals (JSW, Tata Steel) — they sell steel
Falls Autos, consumer durables — they buy steel

05Read this before you trust it

Honest caveats.

  • DATAThe preset numbers are illustrations of regimes, not a live feed. To make this real, wire each slider to an actual metric — inventory / days-of-supply, capacity utilisation, crack spreads, sector-index-vs-Nifty relative strength, % of stocks above their 200-DMA, FII/DII flows.
  • LEADMarkets are forward-looking. By the time scarcity is in the newspaper it’s often already in the price. The value of quantifying is catching the tightening before it’s obvious.
  • TESTA ranking is a hypothesis. Backtest whether overweighting the top decile and underweighting the bottom actually paid, across several cycles, after costs — before you size real positions on it.
  • RISKThis is a research framework for your own thinking — not investment advice, not a recommendation, and no guarantee of returns. Sectors can stay mispriced longer than a model expects.
